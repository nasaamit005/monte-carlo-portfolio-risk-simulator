1. Executive summary

This project uses Monte Carlo simulation to explore a range of possible one-year outcomes

for a hypothetical equal-weighted portfolio of five US technology stocks: AAPL, AMZN,

GOOGL, MSFT, NVDA. It generates 10,000 simulated paths, each spanning 252 trading days,

starting from $10,000.00.

The model estimates a mean terminal value of $13,972.82 and a median terminal value of

$13,440.80. The modeled probability of ending below the initial investment is 14.45%. The

95% VaR loss estimate is $1,452.91, while the 95% Expected Shortfall estimate is $2,323.95.

These are the outputs of the configured model, not predictions or guaranteed investment

outcomes. The numerical values depend on the sample period, return distribution

assumptions, portfolio weights, and random seed.

Here the implementation uses the empirical mean of simulated returns selected into the

lower tail. In a finite sample, the exact number of observations in the tail can vary slightly

due to ties and quantile conventions.

A positive reported loss amount corresponds to a loss threshold or average loss. If the

modeled lower-tail quantile is positive, the signed VaR loss amount can be negative; this is

mathematically possible and should not be silently changed to zero.

VaR limitation: VaR describes a loss quantile but does not indicate how severe losses

can be beyond that quantile. Expected Shortfall complements it by summarizing the

average loss in the selected tail.

2. Sample portfolio paths — demonstrates the range of possible trajectories and how

3. Percentile fan chart — compares daily percentile paths and shows how the range of

modeled outcomes evolves over time.

4. VaR versus Expected Shortfall — compares the loss threshold with the average loss

6. Business and risk-management value

This analysis demonstrates how a quantitative analyst can:

translate historical asset returns into a probabilistic scenario model;

preserve estimated cross-asset correlation in simulated returns;

quantify potential downside using VaR and Expected Shortfall;

estimate the modeled frequency of ending below a capital threshold;

communicate uncertainty through percentile charts and distributions;

produce reproducible, auditable CSV outputs and interactive reports.

A risk manager could use such a framework as an initial scenario-analysis tool, then compare

its estimates with historical stress tests, backtests, and alternative models before using it for

a real risk process.

7. Model limitations and validation work still needed

Normality: Multivariate normal returns can underrepresent fat tails and extreme joint

market moves.

Stationarity: Historical means, volatilities, and correlations may change.

Parameter uncertainty: Estimated means and covariance are not known constants.

Regime changes: The model does not explicitly represent crises or volatility clustering.

Rebalancing assumption: Daily equal-weight rebalancing is assumed; real portfolios

may rebalance less often.

PAGE 08 / 10

Costs: Transaction costs, taxes, market impact, and slippage are excluded.

Concentration: Five technology-heavy stocks can remain highly exposed to common

sector and market risks.

Model validation: No out-of-sample calibration or VaR exceedance backtest is

performed by this script.

Benchmark comparison: The S&P 500 is not included as a portfolio holding in this

Monte Carlo engine.

Recommended next improvements are historical or block bootstrap simulation, Student-t

innovations, volatility models such as GARCH, stress scenarios, and out-of-sample

backtesting.

environments or numerical library versions.
