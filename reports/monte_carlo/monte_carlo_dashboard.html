Monte Carlo Portfolio Risk Dashboard

A scenario-based analysis of a five-stock, equal-weighted portfolio. Historical daily log-return means and covariance are used to simulate correlated asset returns and estimate one-year portfolio outcomes and tail risk.

How to interpret the dashboard

Terminal value distribution

Shows how frequently simulated paths finish at different portfolio values. A wide distribution indicates substantial uncertainty. The initial investment line helps distinguish gains from losses.

Simulated portfolio paths

Shows a sample of individual possible journeys, not a prediction of the actual market path. The paths diverge because daily returns are randomly sampled from the estimated joint distribution.

Percentile fan

The 5th percentile is a modeled lower-tail outcome, the median is the central outcome, and the 95th percentile is an upper-tail outcome. The percentile lines are not confidence intervals for a single realized future path.

VaR and Expected Shortfall

VaR identifies a quantile of the loss distribution. Expected Shortfall averages losses in the tail beyond that quantile. ES helps describe the severity of tail outcomes that VaR alone does not summarize.

Terminal outcome scatter

Each dot represents one simulated outcome. Outcomes below the initial portfolio value are losses; outcomes above it are gains. This chart is useful for seeing the spread and concentration of results.

Equal-weighted portfolio

The model weights the five stocks equally and rebalances daily. It does not include the S&P 500 in the portfolio; the benchmark is not an investable component in this simulation.

Model assumptions and cautions

Daily log returns are simulated from a multivariate normal distribution estimated using historical sample means and covariance. Correlation is represented through the covariance matrix. Simulated log returns are converted to simple returns, and daily portfolio returns are calculated as the weighted sum of constituent simple returns.

These results are conditional model estimates, not guaranteed forecasts. A normal-return model may understate fat tails, volatility clustering, abrupt regime changes, and extreme market events. Historical estimates are uncertain, and transaction costs, taxes, liquidity, and slippage are excluded.
